The Tear of Rudra

A seed with a story

What Rudraksha is, where it grows, and the traditions that have carried it for centuries. Stories here are given as tradition, not as proven fact.

The name

The word Rudraksha comes from Sanskrit: Rudra, a name of Shiva, and aksha, meaning eye, and by extension tear. It is usually translated as "the eye of Rudra" or "the tears of Rudra".

According to Puranic tradition, Shiva meditated for a very long time for the welfare of living beings. When he opened his eyes, tears of compassion fell to the earth, and from them grew the Rudraksha trees.

That is the traditional account. Whatever one believes about the story, it explains why the bead is treated as sacred, and why it is most often associated with Shiva and with meditation.

The tree and the seed

The bead is the hard seed of a tree of the genus Elaeocarpus (the Himalayan species is Elaeocarpus ganitrus). The tree bears a blue fruit, and once the soft flesh is removed, the ridged seed inside is the Rudraksha.

The trees grow in the Himalayan foothills and across parts of South and Southeast Asia. Nepal is one of the places traditionally known for Rudraksha, which is why we source there.

The ridges running from one end of the bead to the other are called mukhi, meaning "faces". Texts commonly describe beads from one to fourteen faces, and the trade lists higher counts too. Each mukhi is associated in tradition with a particular deity and planet.

In practice

How it has been used

As a mala

A traditional mala has 108 beads plus one extra, the guru or bindu bead, which marks the start and end of the count when chanting japa.

As a single bead

A single bead, often a particular mukhi, is worn on a thread or chain, close to the body, in connection with its ruling deity and mantra.

In sadhana

Shaiva ascetics and householders alike have used Rudraksha for japa and meditation. It is also used in some other devotional traditions.

Traditional texts describe many benefits of Rudraksha. These are statements of belief and practice, not scientific or medical claims, and a bead is not a substitute for professional medical, legal or financial advice.
